1. Introduction

1.1 Purpose of this document

This document is the review-ready, decision-grade business plan for SPARTA — the Secure Platform Awareness, Resilience, Trust & Assessment system. It is intended to give the Airbus Defence & Space (Airbus DS) executive board, the FCAS Programme Steering Committee, and Airbus DS Strategy & Finance the analytical evidence they need to authorise (or refuse) Airbus DS investment in SPARTA as a strategic asset inside the FCAS Combat Cloud (Pillar 4) and adjacent platform programmes. It deliberately follows the standard board-review structure — Introduction, Executive Summary, Market Analysis, Capabilities Analysis, Financial Analysis, Compatibility Analysis, Business Requirements, Conclusions — to ensure each load-bearing question a reviewer might ask is answered explicitly in the section where it is conventionally expected.

1.2 Scope and method

The plan is constructed against a CDR-ready technical baseline. SPARTA's mission, architecture, requirements, interfaces, performance budgets, and assurance posture are defined in the SPARTA System Definition Datapack (Deliverables 01–06, Draft C.4, dated 23–24 April 2026). The business plan does not restate that technical baseline; it builds on it. Wherever a strategic claim depends on a specific technical commitment (for example, the seven external interfaces, the seven canonical PBS components, or the 56 SYS-* requirements), the claim is cross-referenced to the relevant datapack section so that the reviewer can audit the chain from business assertion to engineering evidence in a single traversal.

The analytical method blends three review-board-grade frameworks. Market analysis uses Porter's Five Forces and the segmented TAM / SAM / SOM convention used in defence-strategy practice [R-3][R-20][R-25]. Capabilities analysis uses Porter's Value Chain together with the resource-based view of competitive advantage [R-25]. Value proposition design uses the Osterwalder Value Proposition Canvas (customer-side jobs / pains / gains paired with supplier-side pain relievers / gain creators / products and services) [R-26]. Compatibility analysis adapts the standard internal-fit / external-fit / risk taxonomy used by Airbus DS programme reviews. Financial analysis follows the discounted-cash-flow scenario method with explicit single-driver sensitivities. None of these frameworks is novel; they are deliberately conventional so the document is immediately legible to any defence-prime reviewer.

1.3 Conventions and tagging

Every load-bearing factual claim in this plan is tagged with one of three letters that signal the epistemic status of the claim. F marks a verified public fact, anchored on at least one primary source listed in §9. A marks a reasoned assumption — a claim that is not directly verifiable from public sources but that has been constructed by triangulating from public anchors and is explicitly flagged so that Airbus DS Finance can substitute its own internal data when the model is re-run. I marks an expert interpretation — a judgment call that the SPARTA Strategy Cell has formed by integrating multiple sources, and which the reviewer is entitled to dispute. The pragmatic effect of this convention is that the reviewer can re-read the document and recover the analytical chain even if they disagree with a specific judgment. It also ensures the plan does not silently inflate the quality of evidence on which any conclusion rests.

1.4 Audience and decision request

The primary audience is the Airbus DS executive board and the FCAS Programme Steering Committee. The secondary audience is Airbus DS Strategy & Finance, who will be asked to re-run the financial model in §5 against P&L-grade inputs before any external use of the figures. The tertiary audience is the SPARTA technical leadership team, who use this plan as the strategic complement to the technical CDR pack. The decision sought is a clear GO / NO-GO / PIVOT verdict, ideally GO conditional on the five explicit acceptance conditions listed in §8.

1.5 Relation to the SPARTA Datapack

This plan is one of two top-level Airbus-internal SPARTA artefacts. The other is the SPARTA System Definition Datapack, an eight-document HTML datapack covering the technical mission, requirements, architecture, performance, Verification & Validation (V&V), lifecycle, strategic context, and security/safety assurance position of SPARTA at Critical Design Review (CDR)-ready maturity. Where this business plan and the datapack appear to disagree, the datapack is normative on technical claims and this plan is normative on commercial claims. 2. Executive Summary

2.1 Vision

By 2040, Airbus Defence & Space owns the cyber-trust spine of European collaborative combat air. SPARTA is the canonical mission-aware trust object emitted by every Edge node, evaluated by every Fog node (where the post-CDR Mode-B-peer extension is deployed), and adjudicated by every Tactical Security Operations Centre (TSOC) in the Future Combat Air System (FCAS) ecosystem and the adjacent fleets that share its data contract — Eurofighter Long-Term Evolution (LTE), Eurodrone, Multi-Role Tanker Transport (MRTT), A400M, and selected NATO Federated Mission Networking (FMN) partner fleets [R-17] F. The vision is not "build a cyber product"; it is "set the data contract on which Combat Cloud trust depends" and to derive durable architectural authority, recurring services revenue, and political legitimacy from that position.

In ten years, the success state is that no Combat Cloud node in a FCAS-derived programme — manned fighter, Remote Carrier, fog tanker, or ground command post — emits a trust statement that is not in the SPARTA NodeStatus / TrustAssessment schema. In fifteen years, the success state is that signed-content subscription revenue (rules, models, policies, threat profiles, malware verdicts) is large enough to be reported as a discrete services-grade segment line in Airbus SE (Societas Europaea, the parent listed-entity of Airbus Defence & Space) financials, with structurally higher margin than programme-prime defence revenue [R-2] I. The vision is therefore commercial as much as it is strategic: it converts a one-shot platform sale into a recurring trust-content service that compounds over the operational life of every adopting fleet.

This vision is consistent with the published Airbus DS open-architecture thesis as expressed in the Multi-Domain Combat Cloud and MARS reference materials [R-2] F, and with the broader European sovereign-cyber direction reinforced by NIS2, the Cyber Resilience Act, and the European Defence Fund cyber-resilience lines [R-8][R-9][R-12][R-13] F. It is not consistent with a status quo in which third-party vendors (Indra-style sovereign-cyber niches; Thales enterprise SOC product lines; US mission-edge vendors such as Anduril, Palantir, or Shift5) define the cyber-trust data contract while Airbus integrates to it [R-18] I. The vision therefore requires Airbus DS to act in 2026 — before the FCAS Combat Cloud schema freezes — and is the rationale for the financial envelope and acceptance conditions described in §2.4 and §8.

2.2 Opportunity Overview

Three structural shifts in defence aerospace converge on a single engineering problem. First, crewed–uncrewed teaming is no longer aspirational; FCAS, the UK / Italy / Japan Global Combat Air Programme (GCAP), and the US Air Force Collaborative Combat Aircraft (CCA) programme each commit publicly to mixed-formation operations within the 2028–2035 window [R-1][R-10][R-11] F. Second, combat-cloud architectures assume continuous, low-latency, multi-platform data exchange — but the operational environment in which these architectures must function is contested, intermittent, and bandwidth-asymmetric. Third, mission decision rhythm is collapsing toward seconds, while classical fixed-perimeter Security Operations Centres operate on minutes-to-hours [R-3][R-4] F. The combination produces a structural capability gap that none of the publicly disclosed FCAS pillars owns end-to-end: mission-aware cyber trust at the tactical edge that survives DDIL (Disconnected, Degraded, Intermittent, Limited) operating conditions.

The window for closing this gap is open but finite. The FCAS Phase 1B Research & Technology (R&T) demonstrator is contracted (€3.2 B tripartite France / Germany / Spain, December 2022) [R-1][R-7] F, and the demonstrator window 2028–2029 is the schema-freeze period for Combat Cloud data contracts. Whoever's trust object is in the schema at freeze owns the contract for the operational life of FCAS — projected to FCAS Initial Operational Capability (IOC) around 2040 [R-1][R-3] F, with through-life service revenue extending well into the 2050s. The competitive pressure on this window is intensifying. The US Air Force's CCA Increment 1 awards (April 2024) to Anduril and General Atomics [R-10] F and the UK / Italy / Japan Global Combat Air Programme trilateral convention (December 2023) [R-11] F establish parallel programmes that will set NATO-level expectations on edge-cyber. If FCAS does not have a credible mission-aware cyber trust layer by 2028–2029, the de-facto standard will be set elsewhere and Airbus DS will integrate to it.

The regulatory direction of travel reinforces the opportunity. NIS2 [R-12] and the EU Cyber Resilience Act [R-13] F raise the floor on identifiable, auditable, version-controlled cyber telemetry. NATO FMN [R-17] requires coalition-grade interoperability. National sovereign-cyber overlays — DGA-MI in France, BSI and the Cyber-Innovationshub in Germany, CCN-CERT and INCIBE in Spain — sit on top of that floor [R-15][R-16] F. SPARTA's data contract is designed to satisfy these requirements from inception, which converts regulatory pressure from a threat into a tailwind.

2.3 Value Proposition Overview

SPARTA's value proposition is anchored on four properties that are unusual in combination, even though each is individually known. It is mission-aware, not log-aware: it ingests mission role, task and state context from MARS (the Airbus DS on-platform virtualization layer — see Datapack Index §8.1 for the authoritative definition) and uses that context to evaluate the plausibility of observed behaviour, not just the signature of a known attack pattern. It is DDIL-tolerant by design: local detection rate is invariant of link state, so loss of TSOC connectivity converts a delivery problem into a delayed-delivery problem and never produces silent loss. It carries a signed hot-swap content pipeline: rules, IOCs and policies update on operational cadence under a cryptographically signed monotonic chain, separately from the accredited software baseline, which is the same architectural pattern that delivered 30–50% lifecycle-cost reduction on the US FACE and OMS programmes [R-19] A. And it is advisory only by construction: SPARTA never commands. Mission authority resides with Command C2; cyber-response authority with TSOC and Security C2; mission-effect execution with MARS-side host control logic. That partitioning is what makes SPARTA accreditable in jurisdictions where unbounded autonomous cyber actuation is not permitted [R-15][R-16] F.

SPARTA is the cyber-trust spine of the FCAS Combat Cloud. By owning the trust object that every node, sensor and Remote Carrier emits and consumes, Airbus locks in the data contract on which Combat Cloud value depends — and converts a one-shot platform sale into a recurring trust-content service across FCAS, Eurofighter LTE, Eurodrone and partner fleets.

Competitors cannot easily replicate this because replication requires the simultaneous combination of: (a) Combat Cloud prime status (Airbus DS owns Pillar 4 on FCAS) [R-1] F; (b) MARS data-model authority (Airbus DS owns the published open-architecture reference) [R-2] F; (c) operational mission-context access via published MARS interfaces (structural advantage of being inside the platform); and (d) accredited TSOC analyst-workflow ownership via Airbus Protect's Orion Malware® and CymID®-class services [R-2] A. No European or US competitor holds all four positions in FCAS.

3.2 Attractiveness, Technology, Innovation

3.2.1 Market attractiveness

The European defence-cyber market is structurally attractive on five conventional dimensions, anchored on the SIPRI defence-economics literature [R-20][R-24] F and on the EDA Capability Development Plan 2023 revision [R-9] F. First, demand is policy-protected: defence-cyber budget lines are politically defensible across France, Germany and Spain because cyber is treated as sovereign-essential infrastructure, not discretionary investment [R-15][R-16] F. Second, the market is growing in absolute terms — the SIPRI top-100 arms-industry series shows compounded defence-electronics and cyber growth materially above wider European industrial averages over the 2018–2024 period [R-20] F. Third, customer concentration is high — a small number of programmes (FCAS, Eurofighter LTE, Eurodrone, GCAP-equivalent NATO programmes) account for the bulk of opportunities, which keeps sales-and-marketing cost contained. Fourth, switching costs are structurally high once a customer has accredited a particular trust-object schema, because re-accreditation under DGA-MI / BSI / CCN-CERT methodologies takes multi-year effort [R-15][R-16] F. Fifth, regulatory tailwinds — NIS2 and the Cyber Resilience Act — raise the floor on the entire SAM rather than depressing it [R-12][R-13] F.

3.2.2 Technology trajectory

The mission-cyber technology trajectory is shifting from log-based, central, perimeter-oriented Security Operations Centres toward mission-aware, edge-anchored, content-separated cyber architectures. This shift is observable in three publicly traceable streams. The US Department of Defense FACE / OMS work demonstrates the engineering and economic feasibility of content-separated, open-architecture mission systems [R-19] A. The NIST SP 800-160 Vol. 2 cyber-resilience methodology codifies the systems-engineering doctrine for resilient, fault-tolerant cyber-physical systems [R-14] F. The NATO CCD-COE Tallinn Manual line of work codifies the legal and operational doctrine for distributed, accredited, advisory-bounded cyber operations [R-21] F. SPARTA is positioned at the European-sovereign-acceptable intersection of these three streams, which is a defensible standing position for the next ten years.

3.2.3 Innovation envelope

SPARTA's innovation envelope is bounded but high-leverage. The constituent technologies — signature detection, ML anomaly detection, signed-content pipelines, hot-swap atomic updates, Byzantine-tolerant consensus, post-quantum signature profiles — are individually mature and well-described in the public literature [R-14][R-19][R-21]. SPARTA's innovation is in their combination and in the strict authority-partitioned operational doctrine that wraps them. The signed hot-swap content pipeline (SYS-DC-005, realised by F7.4 sub-functions) is the most operationally sensitive innovation: it is the load-bearing mechanism that makes operational-cadence content updates compatible with multi-year accreditation cycles. The advisory-only authority partition (SYS-DC-001) is the most strategically important innovation: it is the design constraint that makes SPARTA accreditable in European jurisdictions where unbounded autonomous cyber actuation is not permitted. Neither innovation is patentable in any conventional sense — but they are nevertheless defensible because they require the structural combination of MARS authority, TSOC integration, and accredited identity provider that only Airbus DS owns in the European context [R-2] I.

3.4 Competitive Forces

Competitive analysis is structured against Porter's Five Forces convention [R-25] F. Each force is rated low / medium / high with a short justification and a SPARTA-specific implication.

3.4.1 Threat of new entrants — medium-high

US-origin mission-edge cyber vendors (Anduril, Palantir, Shift5) operate with software-first cultures, rapid release cadences, and aggressive marketing into NATO contexts [R-18] A. They are not natively MARS-compatible and they face sovereign-acceptability friction in FR / DE / ES, but they could establish a de-facto NATO standard if European primes leave the standard unset for too long. The structural mitigation is anchoring SPARTA into the FCAS Combat Cloud schema before the schema freeze window of 2028–2029 closes, supplemented by EDF-co-funded reference architectures that establish SPARTA as the European sovereign answer [R-8][R-9] F. European defence-cyber start-ups (notably France-based, occasionally Germany-based) are a secondary entrant class; their threat profile is lower than the US class because they typically lack Combat Cloud programme access.

3.4.2 Bargaining power of buyers — medium

Buyers are sovereign customers operating under parliamentary oversight (Bundestag Haushaltsausschuss, Assemblée Nationale Commission de la Défense, Congreso de los Diputados Comisión de Defensa) [R-7] F. They are not price-takers but they are also not maximally price-pressuring; defence-cyber budgets are politically protected because cyber is sovereign-essential infrastructure. The medium rating reflects that sovereign buyers can credibly threaten to develop an in-house alternative, but the structural cost of that threat (multi-year sovereign cyber programmes are expensive and politically exposed) limits how often they can credibly invoke it.

3.4.3 Bargaining power of suppliers — low-medium

SPARTA's external suppliers are commodity-grade in most categories: secure-element / TPM hardware, post-quantum-ready cryptographic libraries, open-source dependencies governed by SBOM and SLSA-3 attestation. The two non-commodity suppliers are Airbus Protect (internal, supplies Orion Malware® verdicts via IF-EXT-06 and CymID®-class identity via IF-EXT-05) and the accredited cross-domain sanitizer vendor (IF-EXT-07). Airbus Protect being internal is a structural advantage; it converts a potential single-point-of-supplier-power into a configurable governance arrangement.

3.4.4 Threat of substitutes — low

Enterprise Security Operations Centre (SOC) and Security Information & Event Management (SIEM) products (Thales CYBELS, Splunk-class enterprise platforms) operate on log-time and are not substitutes for tactical-decision-rhythm mission-aware trust. Per-platform integrity monitors (host-based, signature-driven) are partial substitutes for the F3 detection layer but cannot deliver mission-aware reasoning, peer correlation, or coalition-grade authority partitioning. The structural reason substitute threat is low is that SPARTA's value is in the combination of mission-awareness + DDIL tolerance + signed-content lifecycle + advisory authority partition — not in any single function that could be substituted in isolation.

3.4.5 Industry rivalry — medium

Direct rivals in the European context are Indra (FCAS Pillar 5 sensors prime with sovereign-cyber heritage), Thales (CYBELS cyber-defence product family and Critical National Infrastructure (CNI) cyber heritage), and to a lesser extent Leonardo within GCAP [R-1][R-11] F. Indirect rivals are the US mission-cyber vendors already noted under entrants. The structural reason rivalry is medium rather than high is that none of these rivals simultaneously holds the four positions (Combat Cloud primacy, MARS authority, mission-context access, TSOC analyst-workflow ownership) that SPARTA's defensibility depends on.

6. Compatibility Analysis

Compatibility analysis is structured along three axes: internal fit (does SPARTA align with Airbus DS strategy, capabilities and culture?), external fit (does SPARTA align with the regulatory, standards-based and customer ecosystem?), and risk assessment (what residual risks remain, how are they categorised, who owns mitigation?). The convention follows the standard Airbus DS programme-review compatibility template.

6.1 Internal Fit

6.1.1 Strategic fit with Airbus DS direction

SPARTA aligns with three published elements of Airbus Defence & Space (Airbus DS) strategic direction. First, the Multi-Domain Combat Cloud thesis articulated in Airbus DS public materials since 2018 [R-2] depends on a trust layer to be operationally defensible; SPARTA is that trust layer. Second, the MARS open-architecture commitment — Airbus DS's published reference for combat-cloud-enabled mission systems — needs reference plug-ins to defeat the "open in name only" critique [R-2] I; SPARTA is precisely such a reference plug-in. Third, Airbus DS's stated commitment to sovereign-European cyber capability [R-2] is reinforced by SPARTA's position as a European-headquartered, sovereign-accreditation-acceptable answer to US mission-edge cyber vendors [R-18]. The strategic-fit case is therefore strong.

6.1.2 Capabilities and portfolio fit

SPARTA leverages four existing Airbus DS capabilities that competitors cannot easily replicate. First, the multi-decade systems-engineering heritage in Eurofighter, A400M, Eurodrone, MRTT and Tiger / NH90 (twin-engine NATO-class transport / utility helicopter) mission systems [R-2] provides the engineering depth and accreditation track record on which SPARTA depends. Second, Pillar 4 prime status on FCAS [R-1] gives SPARTA programme-anchored access. Third, the Airbus Protect cyber-services capability provides the operational TSOC backend (Orion Malware®, CymID®) that SPARTA consumes [R-2]. Fourth, the Airbus space and connectivity portfolio (Skynet, Network for the Sky) provides the transport layer over which SPARTA traffic flows. There is no significant capability gap in Airbus DS that SPARTA's success requires building from greenfield — the talent ramp described in §4.1.3 is the principal organic build, and it is bounded at ~63 FTE steady state.

6.1.3 Cultural fit

The principal cultural-fit risk is impedance mismatch between SPARTA's software-product operating cadence (operational, fast-iteration content cadence in days–weeks) and the broader Airbus DS engineering culture (multi-year accreditation cycles, formal milestone-driven contracting). The dual-cadence architecture embedded in the technical baseline (SYS-DC-004 content / software separation) is the structural mitigation: the accredited software baseline follows the slower programme cadence, and the signed-content pipeline runs on the operational cadence familiar to the cyber community. Organisationally, this is implemented by having packages P1, P3, P5, P6 governed under standard Airbus DS programme rules and package P4 governed under a lighter, faster CCB co-chaired by Airbus Protect. The boundary between the two is explicit and contractually defined.

6.1.4 Financial / P&L fit

SPARTA improves the P&L texture of Airbus DS over the horizon. By 2036, recurring services (S3) reach approximately 30% of SPARTA revenue and carry software-services gross margin (55–65%), which is materially higher than programme-prime defence-revenue gross margin [R-2] I. This evolution is consistent with the long-term Airbus SE strategic direction of growing services revenue as a share of total Defence & Space division revenue, observable in the public Annual Report disclosures [R-2] F. The financial-fit case is therefore positive on the long horizon, neutral-to-positive on the near horizon.

6.2 External Fit

6.2.1 Regulatory fit

SPARTA's external regulatory fit is structurally favourable. The NIS2 Directive [R-12] raises the floor on identifiable, auditable cyber telemetry across critical infrastructure operators and supply chains — SPARTA's data contract (NodeStatus, AnomalyEvent, TrustAssessment, ResponseRecommendation, PolicyBundle, EvidencePackage, HealthReport) is designed to satisfy NIS2-style auditability from inception. The EU Cyber Resilience Act [R-13] imposes horizontal cybersecurity requirements on products with digital elements; SPARTA's signed hot-swap content pipeline (SYS-DC-005), supply-chain provenance (SBOM, SLSA-3), and identity / PKI integration (IF-EXT-05) satisfy the load-bearing elements of CRA obligations. The NIST SP 800-160 Vol. 2 cyber-resilience engineering doctrine [R-14] provides the systems-engineering reference against which SPARTA's assurance case is constructed. None of these regulatory frameworks creates a structural incompatibility; they all create demand.

6.2.2 Standards fit

SPARTA's external standards fit is similarly favourable. STANAG 4586 [R-5] for UAV control system interoperability, IEEE 15288 [R-6] for systems-engineering life-cycle processes, and the NATO Federated Mission Networking capability framework [R-17] provide the standards substrate. SPARTA's data contract is designed to be carried over NATO FMN-compliant transports, and its interface schemas (IF-EXT-01..07) are versioned under SYS-IF-005 governance that is forward-compatible by design.

6.2.3 Customer-side fit

The customer-side fit is strong against the sovereign-accreditation customer profile. National accreditation authorities (DGA-MI, ANSSI in France; BSI, Cyber-Innovationshub in Germany; CCN-CERT, INCIBE in Spain) [R-15][R-16] each operate methodologies that explicitly require bounded, auditable, advisory-only cyber behaviour for autonomous platforms; SPARTA's SYS-DC-001 advisory-only constraint and SYS-OPS-004 policy-gated action rule are designed for exactly that customer profile. The fit is somewhat weaker against any customer profile that might demand unbounded autonomous cyber actuation; the deliberate strategic choice has been to forgo that fit in exchange for sovereign-accreditation acceptance, because the European sovereign-accreditation customer profile is the larger and more durable market.

6.2.4 Competitive / ecosystem fit

The competitive ecosystem fit is the most strategically sensitive. SPARTA is designed to be a pre-emptive answer to the prospect that US mission-edge cyber vendors (Anduril, Palantir, Shift5) set a NATO-level standard before FCAS does [R-18] I. The competitive fit relies on three things being true: that the FCAS Combat Cloud schema is not yet frozen (true as of Q2 2026); that Airbus DS retains Pillar 4 prime status (true as of Q2 2026 [R-1]); and that the European Defence Fund continues to fund cyber-resilience lines in its annual Work Programmes (true through the 2025 cycle [R-8]). Each of these conditions has an associated open point in the risk register (§6.3) and an explicit closure criterion.

Residual risk after mitigation is explicit and board-reviewable. The technical CDR closure criterion C-14 (Deliv. 06 §13) requires a signed residual-risk register at CDR gate. For the strategic risk register above, the residual-risk acceptance question for the board is: does the board accept the probability-weighted expected NPV of €233 M (a value-creating outcome) as justifying the residual exposure described in the register, on the conditions stated in §8? The recommendation is that it should — but the decision is the board's.

7.2 Business Constraints

Business constraints are conditions outside SPARTA management control that shape what is and is not achievable. They are categorised below by source.

7.2.1 Programmatic constraints

  • Pillar 4 workshare envelope — SPARTA must operate within the existing Airbus DS Pillar 4 contracted workshare. Any framing of SPARTA as a new pillar would re-open the workshare negotiation that ran 18+ months in 2021–2022 [R-1] and is therefore strictly forbidden.
  • FCAS Industrial Steering rhythm — SPARTA workshare statements, schema decisions and demonstrator gates must align to the FCAS Industrial Steering Committee meeting cadence, which runs on a multi-month rhythm and is structurally not flexible to SPARTA-internal scheduling needs.
  • National budget cycles — FCAS Phase 1B and successor phase funding is voted annually through the French Projet de Loi de Finances (PLF), the German Bundeshaushalt, and the Spanish Presupuestos Generales del Estado (PGE) [R-7]. SPARTA milestone planning must absorb the schedule risk of any single national budget being delayed or reduced.
  • OCCAR contracting model — for Eurodrone-anchored derisking activity, SPARTA must operate within the OCCAR multi-national contracting model rather than bilateral contracting [R-2].

7.2.2 Regulatory constraints

  • NIS2 Directive [R-12] imposes specific auditability, incident-reporting and supply-chain-security requirements that SPARTA must satisfy.
  • EU Cyber Resilience Act [R-13] imposes horizontal cybersecurity requirements on products with digital elements; SPARTA's P1 / P3 / P5 / P6 packages fall within scope.
  • National sovereign-accreditation methodologies [R-15][R-16] — DGA-MI, ANSSI, BSI, CCN-CERT, INCIBE methodologies are non-substitutable; each national customer requires alignment to its own framework.
  • Classification scope — programme classification of the SPARTA datapack is currently tracked as Open Point OP-CLS-01 (Datapack Index §7). The recurring-services content stream (P4) operates under classification-aware release rules that must align to national overlays.
  • Export-control compliance — any extension of SPARTA into the export envelope must satisfy the relevant national export-control regimes (French Direction générale de l'armement (DGA) dual-use controls, German export-licence regime, Spanish Junta Interministerial control). For US technology elements, International Traffic in Arms Regulations (ITAR) or Export Administration Regulations (EAR) compliance may apply if any US-origin components are present in the SPARTA stack.

7.2.3 Financial constraints

  • NRE envelope — €58–88 M over 2026–2038 is the working envelope. Material upward revision requires re-approval from Airbus DS Strategy and Finance, plus revisitation of the Pillar 4 workshare in extreme cases.
  • Recurring operating cost — €4–8 M / year run-rate from 2034 onward must be sustained throughout the operational life of FCAS and adjacent fleets, which extends into the 2050s. This is a multi-decade financial commitment.
  • WACC discipline — model assumes 8.0% nominal WACC per Airbus SE blended average [R-2]. WACC sensitivity is documented in §5.3.3.
  • Foreign exchange exposure — modest, principally EUR/USD on potential US-originated component supply and any NATO FMN export pricing. Sensitivity is +€5% EUR strength → −€11 M NPV (§5.3.3).

7.2.4 Technical constraints (from datapack baseline)

  • CDR-ready Draft C.4 baseline is frozen for the purpose of this plan. Any business decision in this plan must operate within the technical commitments of the SPARTA Datapack Deliverables 01–06 (56 SYS-* requirements, 7 IF-EXT, 7 IF-INT, 7 PBS components, 10-function FBS, NT-01..NT-22 negative tests, C-01..C-15 CDR closure criteria).
  • SYS-DC-001 advisory-only design constraint. Any business proposal that would require SPARTA to assume mission command authority is rejected by the technical constraint and the political mitigation strategy alike.
  • Authority Matrix (Deliv. 06 §7) is normative. Business commitments to customers must be consistent with the Authority Matrix; promises of unbounded autonomous protective action are out of scope.

7.2.5 Political constraints

  • FR / DE / ES political balance — the SPARTA narrative cannot frame Airbus DS as advantaged at the expense of Dassault, Indra or sovereign national interests; framing must be programmatic-cooperative rather than competitive-Airbus-prime.
  • Sovereignty preservation — the per-nation policy-bundle architecture (a single shared data contract supporting multiple per-nation policy bundles) is the technical articulation of the sovereignty constraint. Any business model that requires identical national configurations is non-viable.
  • Dual-use ethics — particularly in Germany, parliamentary scrutiny of dual-use cyber capability under the Bundestag is structurally present. SPARTA's advisory-only design constraint (SYS-DC-001) is the substantive answer to this scrutiny; the explainability commitment (SYS-DATA-003) is the procedural answer [R-15].

8. Conclusions

8.1 Recommendation

Recommendation — GO, conditional

The SPARTA Strategy Cell recommends that the Airbus DS executive board and the FCAS Programme Steering Committee authorise SPARTA investment under the conditions listed below. The recommendation is based on five concurring lines of evidence: a clear and growing structural capability gap in European mission-edge cyber (§2.2, §3.2); a defensible value proposition resting on the four properties that no European or US competitor simultaneously holds (§2.3, §3.1); a market of attractive size, growth, and switching-cost characteristics (§3.2, §3.3); a probability-weighted expected NPV of €233 M against a non-recurring envelope of €58–88 M (§5.3); and a risk profile in which even the downside scenario is value-accretive (§5.3.2, §6.3). The structural asymmetry of outcomes favours GO. The conditional caveat reflects that the financial figures are analyst-modelled and require P&L-grade Finance re-run, and that the strategic anchors in §1–§3 require 2026 source revalidation (OP-STRAT-01).

8.2 Conditions for approval

The five conditions on which the GO recommendation depends are reproduced here in their decision-ready form, each with the named decision authority and the closure deadline.

  1. Workshare statement. A written workshare statement positioning SPARTA inside Combat Cloud (Pillar 4), co-signed at Airbus DS / FCAS Programme level before the next FCAS Industrial Steering Committee. Owner: Programme Sponsor. Deadline: Q2 2026.
  2. Letter of intent on schema inclusion. A letter of intent from the FCAS Combat Cloud architecture board to fold SPARTA trust objects (NodeStatus, AnomalyEvent, TrustAssessment, ResponseRecommendation, PolicyBundle, EvidencePackage, HealthReport, PeerSummary) into the MARS canonical schema before schema freeze. Owner: FCAS Combat Cloud Architecture Board. Deadline: Q3 2026.
  3. Joint demonstration plan with Indra. A signed joint demonstration plan with Indra Sistemas on the TSOC adjudication interface, to neutralise Spanish sovereign-cyber pushback and convert Indra from a potential rival into a downstream complement. Owner: SPARTA Strategy Cell. Deadline: Q3 2026.
  4. Finance re-run. Airbus DS Strategy & Finance to re-run §5 of this plan against P&L-grade inputs and to confirm the recurring-services pricing model. Owner: Airbus DS Strategy & Finance. Deadline: Q4 2026 (concurrent with OP-STRAT-01 closure).
  5. HIL closure of provisional performance values. Closure of provisional SYS-PERF-001..007 and SYS-RAM-004 values on at least one representative node-class HIL (OP-PERF-01). Owner: Chief Systems Engineer. Deadline: T0+5 months (concurrent with CDR window).

8.3 Decision request to the board

The decision request to the Airbus DS executive board is a single binary verdict: GO conditional on the five conditions above, or NO-GO. A third option — PIVOT — would be triggered only if conditions (1) or (2) cannot be met by Q3 2026, in which case the pivot would be to anchor SPARTA on Eurodrone and Eurofighter LTE in parallel and defer the FCAS Combat Cloud anchoring to a later schema-revision cycle. The PIVOT case retains a positive NPV (close to the downside scenario of §5.3.2, approximately €54 M) but loses most of the strategic moat. The recommendation is strongly that the board choose GO conditional.

8.4 Closing observation

The pragmatic case for SPARTA is not that it is a guaranteed revenue stream — defence-cyber programmes never are. The pragmatic case is that it is the cheapest available insurance policy against a worse outcome: a future in which Airbus DS is the integrator to a cyber-trust data contract that a third-party vendor defined, in a Combat Cloud whose value depends on that contract, on platforms that Airbus DS otherwise leads. The strategic asymmetry of acting now versus deferring is large and one-directional. The board should authorise GO conditional, on the five conditions stated, and direct the SPARTA Strategy Cell to deliver the conditions by the Q3 2026 inflexion point.
