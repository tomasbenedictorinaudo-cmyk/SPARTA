1. Introduction

This section explains what the document is, how it is written, and what decision it supports. The reader will find here the scope, the drafting conventions used throughout the plan, and the specific board decision the analysis is designed to inform. The main takeaway is that the plan asks the board to approve nine million euros of near term investment to reach a Bundeswehr demonstrator in early 2027, staged behind explicit external triggers so that the worst case downside is capped near 1.25 million euros.

1.1 What this document is

SPARTA is a piece of software that runs on military platforms such as drones and combat aircraft. Its role is to detect cyber attacks against the platform in near real time, to decide how much the platform can still be trusted, and to share that judgement with the ground command post. This document sets out the business case for building SPARTA. The decision it supports is whether Airbus Defence and Space should invest in the product, and if so, at what level and on what timetable.

1.2 How this document is written

Three drafting habits apply throughout. The first is plain language. Every technical or financial term is defined the first time it appears, and every acronym is expanded in the glossary in section 9. The second habit is transparent arithmetic. Every quantitative claim is traceable to its inputs through a short calculation block, so that a reviewer can substitute their own figures and re run the same computation. The third habit is source anchoring. Every load bearing factual claim is referenced against a numbered primary source in section 10. Analyst assumptions are flagged explicitly wherever they appear so that the reader can distinguish evidence from judgement.

1.3 What we are asking

The board is asked to authorise two things in sequence. The first is a near term commitment of nine million euros to build a working Minimum Viable Product and demonstrate it to the German armed forces in the first quarter of 2027. The second is a conditional envelope of 49 to 79 million euros of further spending over the following twelve years, released tranche by tranche only if a sequence of external triggers fire in order. Those triggers are the Bundeswehr demonstrator, the German BSI accreditation, and a first paying customer in operations.

The expected return is a Net Present Value of 232 million euros in the base case. Net Present Value is the size of the profit an investment creates over its horizon, expressed in today's euros after future cash flows are discounted at the cost of capital. The full workings for the 232 million figure appear in section 5.3. The probability of a value creating outcome is 95 percent. The worst case downside is a loss of about 1.25 million euros if the earliest external trigger fails.

2.1 Vision

Modern military platforms are increasingly software defined and networked. That evolution makes them easier to upgrade and easier to team into fleets. It also opens them to cyber attacks in ways older platforms never faced. A ground based Security Operations Centre of the kind that defends corporate information technology cannot keep pace with the decision rhythm of a drone in flight. Cyber decisions in combat need to happen in seconds, and they need to happen on the platform itself.

SPARTA is the Airbus Defence and Space answer to that requirement. It is a small software layer that runs inside the platform. It watches the vehicle for signs of cyber attack, decides how much the platform can still be trusted, and reports that decision to the operator on board and to the ground command post. When the radio link is weak or lost, it continues to operate locally and stores its evidence to send back when the link returns.

The vision is that Airbus Defence and Space becomes the reference vendor of embedded cyber defence for European military platforms. By 2029, SPARTA is in operational use on the Bundeswehr fleet. By 2035, SPARTA is the trust layer inside every FCAS Combat Cloud platform. By 2040, the recurring services part of SPARTA revenue, which sells rule updates and threat content on subscription, is worth roughly the same as the direct platform revenue.

The operating doctrine is simple, and the plan repeats it deliberately because it is the argument on which everything else depends. Get a foot in the door first, then expand. A working system in operational use on a real fleet in 2029 is worth more, strategically and commercially, than a fuller system on paper in 2032.

2.2 Opportunity Overview

This subsection sets out why the market is prime now. Four separate forces, each of which stands on its own, converge to make the timing urgent. The takeaway is that a vendor that cannot show a working system on a real fleet by 2029 will not make the shortlist for the procurements that follow.

Ongoing conflicts raise the need for autonomy

The war in Ukraine and the growing tensions on the eastern flank of NATO have caused European militaries to increase their spending on unmanned systems sharply. Public analyst forecasts converge on the same view. The European military drone market roughly doubles by 2030, reaching about 20 billion euros in annual value, as reported in reference R-1 and R-27. The Total Addressable Market for the specific cyber trust layer that SPARTA addresses is 6 to 10 billion euros cumulative over 2026 to 2040. The Serviceable Available Market is 2 to 4 billion euros. The share that Airbus can realistically capture is 0.5 to 1 billion euros. Section 3.3 shows the arithmetic behind each of these figures.

Vehicles are becoming software defined and networked

Every platform now runs software, and every software surface is a possible entry point for an attacker. The more software that is running on the platform, the larger the surface an attacker can hit. A drone in flight can be attacked over its data link, its GPS signal, its sensor buses, and the software supply chain that provided its firmware. Each of these routes requires monitoring in real time. Ground based cyber tools cannot perform that role because they are too far away and too slow.

Regulation raises the floor and cyber budgets are politically protected

The European Union has adopted two pieces of law that matter directly for this market. NIS2 is Directive 2022 slash 2555, referenced as R-12. It requires organisations that run essential systems to have cyber security governance in place and to maintain auditable records of what those systems do. The Cyber Resilience Act sets horizontal cyber security rules on every product with digital elements sold in the EU, referenced as R-13. Both instruments raise the compliance floor across the whole market. As a result, the customer already knows they need something like SPARTA, so the buying question is which vendor rather than whether to buy at all. National defence cyber budgets in France, Germany and Spain are treated as sovereign essential and survive fiscal pressure better than other defence lines, as documented in references R-15 and R-16. Autonomous platforms, and drones in particular, are the specific category into which the money is flowing.

The competitive clock is ticking

Three named competitors are already moving into this space. Helsing.AI is a Munich based defence AI start up founded in 2021. It has raised significant venture capital and is winning Bundeswehr contracts today, as reported in reference R-18. Palantir is a US mission data integrator whose Foundry and Gotham platforms are already deployed in several NATO militaries, also documented in R-18. Indra is the Spanish FCAS Pillar 5 sensors prime and holds a sovereign cyber heritage in Spain, referenced in R-1. Any one of them could set a de facto NATO standard for platform cyber trust if Airbus DS moves too slowly.

Market is prime now, service must begin by 2029

The buyer wants a solution now, not in ten years. A vendor that cannot show a working, operational system on a real fleet by 2029 will not make the shortlist for the procurements that follow. This is the reason the plan treats 2029 as the operational deadline for the whole programme.

Market growth

The dual cadence discipline

The value chain runs on two clocks side by side. The accredited software baseline runs on the slow programme clock, which is measured in years.
